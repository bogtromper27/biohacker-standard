What a Certificate of Analysis actually proves — and what it doesn't

A COA is the single most-cited proof of quality in the supplement and peptide world. Here's how to read one properly — and the gaps a certificate can quietly leave open.

A Certificate of Analysis — a COA — is what a seller hands you to prove that what's on the label is what's in the product. In a market where anyone can print "99% pure" on a bottle, it's the closest thing to hard evidence you'll get. But a COA only means something if you can read it, and most people skim it for a single number and move on.

This is the guide we wish existed when we started reading lab reports. It won't make you a chemist. It will let you tell a real certificate from a reassuring-looking PDF in about ninety seconds.

What a COA actually is

A COA is a laboratory's report on one specific batch of a product. That last part matters more than anything else on the page: a certificate describes the sample the lab tested, not the bottle in your hand. If the batch numbers don't match, the document is decoration.

A complete certificate answers three questions: is it the right compound (identity), how much of it is there (purity/potency), and what else is in there (contaminants). Reputable labs run these against published methods rather than house rules.1

The three panels that matter

1. Identity

Identity testing confirms the substance is what it claims to be — usually by mass spectrometry or a comparison against a reference standard. Without it, purity is meaningless: you can have a 99%-pure sample of the wrong molecule.

2. Purity and potency

This is the number everyone quotes. It should state the assay method and the measured result, not just a marketing figure. A purity claim with no method listed is a claim, not a measurement.

3. Contaminants

The panel people skip and shouldn't. Depending on the product, this covers elemental impurities such as heavy metals,23 residual solvents left over from manufacturing,4 and microbial contamination.5 These are the results that speak to safety rather than marketing.

A certificate proves what a lab measured on one batch — not what's in the bottle on your shelf.

Where COAs quietly mislead

A document can be technically real and still tell you very little. The common gaps:

  • Batch mismatch. The COA is genuine but describes a different lot than the one you received. Always match the batch number.
  • In-house, not independent. A result from the seller's own lab isn't the same as one from a third-party facility whose competence is externally accredited.1
  • Cherry-picked panels. A glowing purity number with no contaminant testing at all is a selective disclosure.
  • No method, no date. A credible certificate names its methods and is recent. Undated or method-less reports can't be verified.
  • Good manufacturing gaps. A single COA says nothing about whether the facility follows current good manufacturing practice across production.6
Key takeaways
  • A COA describes one batch — match the batch number first.
  • Look for all three panels: identity, purity, and contaminants.
  • Prefer third-party, accredited labs over in-house results.
  • No method and no date means the result can't be verified.

The 90-second check

Run any certificate through this before you trust it. The order matters — each step gates the next.

  1. Match the batch. Does the lot number on the COA match the product? If not, stop here.
  2. Find the lab. Is it a named third-party lab, and is it accredited? An in-house result is a weaker signal.
  3. Check the panels. Identity, purity, and contaminants should all be present — not just the flattering one.
  4. Read the methods. Each result should name the method used. "99% pure" with no method is marketing.
  5. Check the date. Recent and signed. Old or undated certificates get re-verified before you rely on them.

Five questions for any vendor

If a seller can't answer these without friction, that's your answer:

  • Can I see the COA for this batch, not a representative one?
  • Which third-party lab ran it, and are they accredited?
  • Does testing include a contaminant panel, or only purity?
  • What methods were used for identity and assay?
  • How often do you re-test across production runs?

None of this requires trusting us. It requires the seller to show their work — which is the entire point of a standard.
